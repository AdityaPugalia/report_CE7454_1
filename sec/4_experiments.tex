\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\noindent\textbf{Protocol.} Clean and simulated accuracies are measured on the 3,000 held-out images, and dev accuracy on 900 images (\cref{sec:v4}). Test accuracy comes from the leaderboard and exists only for submitted models. For the others we report how many test images are predicted real (500 of 1,500 expected). Every model runs once on the unmodified $512^2$ image.

\subsection{Main result}
The baseline is perfect on clean and dev data but scores 34.4\% on the simulator (\cref{tab:main}). Our model gives up 0.3 points of clean accuracy and scores 99.6\% on the simulator, 96.1\% on dev and \textbf{95.3\%} (1,429/1,500) on test. Dev predicted the test score closely. The simulator overestimated it by four points, because it reproduces the processing but not the change of scene content in the hidden sets.

\subsection{What does our model read differently?}
\Cref{fig:probes} perturbs the held-out images without retraining.
\emph{Lattice phase.} The baseline drops to chance for every shift that is not a multiple of 8 and for flips and rotations. These move or mirror the lattice without removing it, which confirms the diagnosis. Our model is unaffected (99.7\%).
\emph{Scale.} A $\pm10\%$ rescale is fatal for the baseline (33\%) and costs our model 8--12 points, although such factors occur in training. Halving the resolution, outside the training range, leaves our model at 80.9\%. The cue is partly scale-dependent: a 0.8\% stretch is nearly free, a 10\% one is not.
\emph{Compression and noise.} JPEG q50 costs our model 2 points (baseline: 53), and Gaussian noise with $\sigma{=}5$ costs it 3.5 points (baseline: 36). The one cost of robustness is blur: $\sigma{=}1$ costs our model 2.3 points and the baseline 0.1.

\subsection{Ablations and training behaviour}
\noindent\textbf{Broad beats matched augmentation.} Augmentation restricted to the neighbourhood of the simulated processing gives nearly the same simulator accuracy (99.2\%) but 4.5 points less on dev, and it predicts 660 test images as real. Matching the simulator teaches the model the simulator. The broad ranges of our augmentation regularise against what the simulator misses.

\noindent\textbf{Crop size and class rebalancing.} A variant trained with $128^2$ crops and 20 copies of the smoothest 25\% of real images ties our model (dev 96.0\%). Its best-dev checkpoint (97.3\%, epoch 20) predicts 744 test images as real, so on 900 dev images a 1-point gain is noise. We therefore select on harsh validation.

\noindent\textbf{Invariance is slow to learn.} The baseline passes 99.9\% after two epochs of 12k images. Our model needs 13 epochs of 152k crops to pass 98\% on the simulator (\cref{fig:curves}): invariance must be learnt from many shifted and rescaled examples. The held-out curves track training accuracy, so 30 epochs do not overfit.

\noindent\textbf{An excluded alternative.} Shrinking test images to $508^2$ and pooling a phase-locked model over 64 lattice shifts scores 100\% on the simulator. It uses a test-derived constant and combines 64 predictions per image, which the rules forbid, so we did not submit it.

\subsection{Error analysis}
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/failures.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Most confident errors of our model on dev}: real photographs labelled as Model~A or~B with probability ${>}0.99$. Each scene is ordinary and textured; none shows visible processing.}
  \label{fig:failures}
\end{figure}
The dev confusion matrix of our model (rows: true real/A/B) is
$\left[\begin{smallmatrix}268&10&22\\2&298&0\\1&0&299\end{smallmatrix}\right]$.
32 of its 35 errors are real images called fake. A and B are never confused with each other, and fake recall is 99.5\%. Real recall rises with texture, from 84\% for the smoothest quarter of real images to 92--93\% for the two most textured. Yet the most confident errors (\cref{fig:failures}) are ordinary textured photographs, which suggests our model partly relies on scene statistics of the training reals. On test, our model predicts 36 more real images than exist, so some fakes, plausibly those weakened by re-encoding, are called real. Either way, the errors are real versus fake, not A versus B.
